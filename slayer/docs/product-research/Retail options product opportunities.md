# Sell the Receipt, Not the Signal

The product with the best evidence behind it that ThetaData Pro and Unusual Whales could power is not another flow tape or gamma dashboard. It is a **verification layer** for the retail options trader. Its parts are an audit of each fill against the NBBO in force at that millisecond, a Greek-level "autopsy" that separates spread paid from being wrong, a replay simulator on recorded tick data, and a checker that asks whether an advertised track record could actually have been traded. The academic record explains why. Retail option buyers lose money mostly to execution cost, not bad direction calls: in the main study, **$6.4 billion of spread cost against a $2.1 billion net loss**. No US rule gives options traders the fill-quality statistics that equity traders get. The tool market splits into about fifteen paid products selling flow, GEX and volatility views. I found none that audits fills, splits spread from P&L, backtests against random baselines or puts error bars on dealer positioning. These ideas also cost the least to license. Neither of the owner's current keys can legally power any paid product, including scores derived from the data. But a product built only on prior-session ("historical") OPRA data owes **no OPRA redistribution fee**. On ThetaData's posted commercial prices that puts the data cost at roughly **$1,250–$3,200 a month**. A real-time product needs about **$5,000–$9,000 a month plus about $1.25 per user**. Three caveats apply. ThetaData has not published its terms for showing data to customers. Trader-level data suggest that the engaged traders who pay for tools may lose much less to spreads than the aggregate figures imply. And demand for retail options tools rises and falls with market activity.

## Retail traders lose to the spread, not the forecast

The best-documented fact about retail options losses is that they come mostly from trading costs. Bryzgalova, Pavlova and Sikorskaya studied retail options trades from November 2019 to June 2021, identified through wholesaler price-improvement prints on OPRA. Over a 10-day holding horizon, retail lost **$2.1 billion**. Effective spread costs over the same period were **$6.4 billion**, against about $0.9 billion in commissions. The authors conclude that "the bulk of the losses comes from the indirect costs of trading," costs that "are likely to be overlooked by retail investors." Retail favours cheap weekly contracts with an average quoted spread of **12.6%**, half of retail trades are in options with under a week to expiry, and some of those carry spreads near 29% ([Journal of Finance, 2023](https://lbsresearch.london.edu/id/eprint/2827/1/The%20Journal%20of%20Finance%20-%202023%20-%20BRYZGALOVA%20-%20Retail%20Trading%20in%20Options%20and%20the%20Rise%20of%20the%20Big%20Three%20Wholesalers.pdf)). Valued at the midquote, the aggregate retail book would have been roughly break-even. That is my arithmetic, not the authors' claim, but it suggests timing and stock-picking were not the problem. The 0DTE evidence points the same way. Beckmeyer, Branger and Gayda found retail SPX 0DTE losses of about $70 million, **more than $50 million of it transaction costs**. Retail timing roughly tracked how expensive the options were, and retail selling was profitable ([CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=44172); [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2023-04-21/day-traders-lose-358-000-per-day-gambling-on-zero-day-options)).

A second, separate loss source is **overpaying for event volatility**. De Silva, Smith and So find that retail buys options before earnings announcements, overpays relative to realized volatility, pays spreads near 20% of the option price, and holds for weeks after the IV crush. Losses average 5–9%, and 10–14% around high-uncertainty announcements ([AFA listing](https://afajof.org/management/viewp.php?n=122472); [Quantpedia summary](https://quantpedia.com/how-retail-losses-money-in-option-trading/)). The popular "lottery preference" story mostly explains which contracts retail picks: cheap, short-dated and out of the money, which are also the costliest to trade. It does not appear as lottery-like realized payoffs ([Bogousslavsky & Muravyev](https://www.lsu.edu/business/files/event-files/2025-finance-mardi-gras/retail_option_trading_v2.pdf)). All three major datasets agree on one pattern: **buyers lose and sellers win**.

There is a strong counter-argument. Bogousslavsky and Muravyev used verified broker-linked journal data covering 5,182 traders and 0.9 million option trades. They find the average option trade loses only **−0.9%**, small next to typical 5–10% spreads, and they suspect these traders use limit orders. Losses concentrate in 0DTE, at **−4.6% per trade** with 93% opened by a purchase, and among small traders at −3.4% ([Bogousslavsky & Muravyev](https://www.lsu.edu/business/files/event-files/2025-finance-mardi-gras/retail_option_trading_v2.pdf)). This is the most important caveat in this report. The engaged, tool-buying trader may already avoid much of the spread cost. The traders who bleed most, small accounts using market orders on weeklies, are the least likely to pay $50 a month for analytics. A cost-transparency product therefore needs a free tier that reaches the bleeders, and it must show measured savings for the sophisticated users.

The disclosure gap is structural. Options payment for order flow is concentrated and lucrative. In 2021 brokers earned $2.4 billion in options PFOF against $1.3 billion for equities ([JF 2023](https://lbsresearch.london.edu/id/eprint/2827/1/The%20Journal%20of%20Finance%20-%202023%20-%20BRYZGALOVA%20-%20Retail%20Trading%20in%20Options%20and%20the%20Rise%20of%20the%20Big%20Three%20Wholesalers.pdf)), and Q2 2025 rates were about **$0.56 per contract at Robinhood versus $0.40 at Schwab** ([GlobalTrading](https://www.globaltrading.net/schwab-overtakes-robinhood-in-options-pfof-as-per-contract-rates-diverge/)). Ernst and Spatt find that market makers who pay for flow give retail options orders less price improvement. Per dollar invested, options can earn a broker about **10× the revenue of equities** ([NBER w29883](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29883/w29883.pdf); [CMU Tepper, 2026](https://www.cmu.edu/tepper/news/stories/high-option-trading-fees-barriers-competition)). The amended SEC Rule 605 execution-quality reports apply to NMS stocks; the proposal "contain[ed] no mention of options" ([Seward & Kissel](https://sewkis.com/?p=14103); [SEC exemptive order 2026](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/exorders/2026/34-105136.pdf)). Rule 606 discloses where orders were routed and what the broker was paid, not what the customer received ([InvestmentNews](https://www.investmentnews.com/regulation-legal-compliance/sec-requires-broker-dealers-to-disclose-more-about-client-orders/76825)). A retail options trader cannot check their own fill quality from any mandated disclosure. Retail traders want that check: one r/options user compared thinkorswim and Robinhood options fills by hand ([r/options mirror](https://axebps-redlib.hf.space/r/options/comments/1jxsg0n/deep_dive_into_tos_vs_robinhood_execution_quality)).

Two regulatory trends make demand likely to grow. On April 14, 2026 the SEC approved FINRA's replacement of the $25,000 pattern-day-trader minimum with intraday margin standards. Firms have until October 20, 2027 to implement it ([SEC approval order](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/sro/finra/2026/34-105226.pdf); [Schwab](https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/sec-approves-scrapping-25000-day-trader-minimum)). That opens day-trading to exactly the small accounts that lose most per trade. Meanwhile FINRA's 2026 investor alert on 0DTE stresses risk rather than cost ([FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/zeroing-in-options-trading-strategy)), so no regulator is yet telling traders what the spread is costing them. Causal evidence that showing costs changes behaviour is thin. I found no US randomized trial. Korea's mandatory-education regime cut participation more than it demonstrably cut losses ([Korea Times](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/economy/20260423/korea-preps-single-stock-leveraged-etfs-as-experts-question-investor-safeguards)). Any product that promises to "save you money" should therefore measure that claim on its own users and publish the result.

Three things are absent across the whole table. First, no retail vendor publishes a verified out-of-sample track record. The industry pattern is a "data provider, not stock picks" disclaimer next to performance-flavoured marketing ([FlowAlgo](https://www.flowalgo.com/); [InsiderFinance](https://www.insiderfinance.io/pricing)). Second, no GEX product shows error bars, even though every published GEX inherits an unobserved sign convention. A sign error inverts the long-gamma and short-gamma regimes ([Chilingarian, SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7131778)), and the trade-direction rules used to sign flow are right only **59–83%** of the time. Index complex-trade legs are misclassified about half the time ([JFQA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/on-inferring-the-direction-of-option-trades/FDA4541B57F78B2C8DCE129AFC25AAF0)). Third, nothing checks an options claim against the actual tape. Kinfo verifies through broker connections, and Collective2 calls its own records "hypothetical" ([Kinfo](https://kinfo.com/verified-trades); [Collective2](https://trade.collective2.com/)). For options replay, the closest product, OptionNet Explorer, runs on 5-minute data ([DayTrading.com](https://www.daytrading.com/optionnet-explorer)). TradingSim's tick replay with real bid/ask covers stocks, futures and crypto but not options ([TradingSim](https://www.tradingsim.com/trading-simulator)).

The complaints users make match these gaps. Users complain about alerts that lag or fire after the move, noisy "unusual" flags, prices out of line with cheaper look-alikes, and toxic Discord communities ([Trustpilot](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/blackboxstocks.com)). The signal edge itself is fading. Strong's *Journal of Portfolio Management* study finds that properly defined unusual activity in near-expiry OTM calls did predict returns, but **profitability has declined since 2020** as the strategy became widely followed, and large trades in general are not predictive ([PM Research](https://www.pm-research.com/content/iijpormgmt/52/9/257)). I found no academic study of whether following a retail flow-alert service is profitable after costs. That fits the owner's own finding of no validated intraday edge. Another signal product would enter a crowded, decaying market. A product that measures and verifies would enter an empty one.

The business record is sobering too. The only public pure-play, BlackBoxStocks, saw revenue fall from **$4.96 million (2022) to $3.1 million (2023)**, carried a going-concern warning, and attempted a reverse merger with an aluminium-parts maker ([FY2023 results](https://www.fintechfutures.com/press-releases/blackboxstocks-announces-financial-results-for-the-fourth-quarter-and-year-ended-december-31-2023); [10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1567900/000143774924010302/blkbx20231231_10k.htm)). The large exits went to platforms with accounts and execution, not to analytics: IG–tastytrade at about $1 billion and Kraken–NinjaTrader at $1.5 billion ([Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20210120005810/en); [Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250320152798/en/Kraken-to-Acquire-NinjaTrader-Introducing-the-Next-Era-of-Professional-Trading?stream=top)). The model that holds up best is Unusual Whales' two-sided business. Retail subscriptions build the brand, and the data is resold through API and enterprise licences to brokers, desks and AI firms ([UW Enterprise](https://unusualwhales.com/enterprise)).

## Neither current key can power a product; historical data is the cheap door

Both keys the owner holds are personal-use licences, and both forbid exactly what a product does. ThetaData's subscriber agreement limits use to "internal personal use". It prohibits displaying "any compilation or directory based upon information derived from the Data" and creating derivative works. It also states that "derived works generated from Theta Data cannot be redistributed before or after the time of termination", and derived works must be deleted within 30 days of termination ([ThetaData Subscriber Agreement](https://www.thetadata.net/subscriber-agreement)). Under these terms even a single aggregated score (a "spread tax" figure, a GEX number) shown to a paying user is a breach. A research prototype built on the personal key should not simply be switched on for customers; it should be rebuilt or re-licensed under a commercial agreement. The Unusual Whales API is "strictly for personal use" and "Redistribution is not allowed"; violators are terminated without refund ([UW Public API](https://unusualwhales.com/public-api)). Any commercial use requires the Startup tier ($750 a month, or $625 billed annually), which permits "personal or commercial use". Redistribution to end users is offered only under custom Professional & Enterprise, "upon approval" ([UW Enterprise](https://unusualwhales.com/enterprise)). Read strictly, even using UW Basic for internal R&D on a commercial product falls outside "personal use".

The OPRA layer sits on top of any vendor. A firm that shows OPRA data to others becomes an OPRA Vendor. It pays a **$1,500 monthly redistribution fee** ($650 for query-only with no auto-refresh) and **$1.25 per non-professional user** (tiering down to $0.60 above 500,000 users) or **$31.50 per professional device**. Separately, non-display use such as computing analytics for clients carries fees of $2,000 per category ([OPRA Fee Schedule](https://cdn.opraplan.com/documents/OPRA_Fee_Schedule.pdf)). Two definitions in that schedule shape the whole strategy. Data becomes "delayed" after 15 minutes. Delayed data drops the per-user fees, but the primary text applies the redistribution fee to data furnished "on a current or delayed basis." ThetaData's own fee guide says there are no OPRA fees for data over 15 minutes old. The primary schedule is the safer reading ([ThetaData OPRA guide](https://thetadata.net/articles/2026-05-29-opra-fee-guide-for-options-market-data)). Data becomes "historical" **"upon the opening of trading on the next succeeding trading day"**, and a vendor whose redistribution "is limited solely to 'historical' OPRA Data" is exempt from the redistribution fee ([OPRA Fee Schedule](https://cdn.opraplan.com/documents/OPRA_Fee_Schedule.pdf)). So a fill audit, attribution report or replay delivered after the next morning's open owes OPRA nothing. OPRA publishes **no policy on derived data** such as greeks, IV or GEX ([OPRA FAQ](https://opraplan.com/faqs)). The equity-side CT Plan approved in June 2026 now treats creating derived data as fee-liable non-display use. It also makes delayed and end-of-day stock data non-billable, and its non-pro rate starts at $0.90 per tape ([SEC Release 34-105778](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/sro/nms/2026/34-105778.pdf); [Federal Register](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-04-03/pdf/2026-06463.pdf)).

The vendor side is where the real uncertainty lies. ThetaData's commercial plans list **options at $1,600 a month (startups "as low as $500"), stocks at $1,200 ($500), and indices at $400 ($250)**. The page does not say whether these include display or redistribution rights, and "redistribution… is prohibited without a commercial agreement that permits it" ([ThetaData Commercial Use](https://www.thetadata.net/commercial-use); [ThetaData OPRA guide](https://thetadata.net/articles/2026-05-29-opra-fee-guide-for-options-market-data)). Index levels such as SPX and VIX spot are licensed separately by their owners, and current per-user fees are not posted. A small firm will normally take them through a vendor's pass-through ([S&P DJI](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/about-us/data-index-licensing); [Cboe DataShop](https://datashop.cboe.com/sip-fees)). The bring-your-own path exists but has not been checked. Tradier, for example, supplies real-time data only to its own brokerage accounts ([Sentisense](https://sentisense.ai/blog/unusual-whales-api-alternatives-options-flow-apis-2026/)). Whether Schwab, IBKR or tastytrade let a third-party app display their quotes was not researched and has to be confirmed broker by broker. The practical conclusion is that the flagship features should be designed around next-morning delivery. The ideas with the best evidence happen to need nothing faster.

Securities law adds a second set of design rules. A subscription analytics product normally avoids Investment Advisers Act registration under the publisher's exclusion of *Lowe v. SEC* (1985). The content must be impersonal, bona fide and regularly published ([472 U.S. 181](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-472/pdf/USREPORTS-472-181.pdf)). The exclusion was lost where a firm used questionnaires to tailor advice and auto-traded subscriber accounts (*Weiss Research*, 2006). In 2024, alerts that merely filter content every subscriber can see stayed inside it (*Seeking Alpha*) ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2024/8/no-need-for-seeking-alpha-to-seek-registration)). The FTC has already acted against options-alert sellers' earnings claims: RagingBull settled for **$2.425 million** and Warrior Trading for **$3 million** ([FTC](https://www.consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2022/03/investment-trading-operation-will-pay-millions-empty-promises); [FTC business blog](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2022/04/ftc-bids-good-night-deceptive-day-trading-earnings-claims)). A verification product sits on the safe side of both lines, with one exception. An AI feature that says "given your positions, buy X" turns impersonal content into personal advice. Adviser status would also reclassify staff and possibly users as data "professionals" at $31.50 rather than $1.25 ([OPRA FAQ](https://opraplan.com/faqs)).

### The fill audit is the flagship because it targets the largest measured cost

The user imports fills from a broker CSV or API. Each fill is matched to the NBBO at its timestamp, and where possible to the user's own print in the OPRA tape, which recovers millisecond timing that many broker exports lack. The tool then reports the effective spread, price improvement in dollars and as a share of the half-spread, where the fill landed inside the quote, 1-, 5- and 60-second markouts, and a running "you paid $X in spread this year". ThetaData supplies everything. Its trade-quote endpoint returns "every trade reported by OPRA paired with the last NBBO quote at the time of trade" ([ThetaDataDx reference](https://userfrm.github.io/ThetaDataDx/reference/option/history/trade-quote)). Unusual Whales is not needed. The product works fully as a next-morning report, so it falls under OPRA's historical exemption. The defensible asset is the dataset, not the lookup. Aggregated fills would produce the options execution league table that Rule 605 does not, for example median price improvement on SPY 0DTE by broker. That table is also the distribution engine, mirroring how Unusual Whales built distribution through free newsletter, podcast and syndicated content ([UW Substack](https://unusualwhales.substack.com/p/unusual-whales-api-prices-increasing); [Nasdaq author page](https://www.nasdaq.com/authors/unusual-whales/page/3)). Monetization runs in three layers: a free summary to reach the small market-order traders who lose most, a paid tier priced near journals, and B2B licensing to advisers documenting best execution, to researchers and to the press. Two risks must be designed in. The crowdsourced sample is self-selected, Robinhood's own "order mix" objection ([Robinhood policy blog](https://robinhood.com/us/en/policy/blog/rule-605-and-the-limits-of-efficacy/)), so comparisons need adjusting for spread width, DTE and order type. And fills in busy contracts with many identical prints cannot be matched to the millisecond.

### Autopsy and replay turn the same tape into explanation and practice

The autopsy splits each holding period into one-minute steps using ThetaData's greek history. ThetaData offers per-trade greeks up to third order ([ThetaDataDx greeks](https://userfrm.github.io/ThetaDataDx/reference/option/history/trade-greeks/all)). P&L is attributed to delta, gamma, theta and vega, with spread paid as its own line and a residual. The output is a sentence like "you were right on direction but lost 70% to IV crush and the spread". That speaks directly to the de Silva finding on holding through the crush. Reviews credit TraderSync and TradesViz with greek-level P&L, but neither is described as isolating spread ([JournalPlus](https://journalplus.co/compare/journalplus-vs-trademetria-vs-tradesviz)). The maths is textbook, so the autopsy belongs inside the fill-audit subscription, not on its own. Users should be told that ThetaData's greeks are Black-Scholes model outputs, and that index-option greeks only begin in 2017 ([ThetaData](https://thetadata.net/options-data); [ThetaData docs](https://docs.thetadata.us/Articles/Data-And-Requests/Data-Issues.html)).

The replay simulator streams a recorded session's one-second NBBO and prints at adjustable speed. Market orders fill at the quote plus latency, and limit orders fill only when a real print trades through the limit. Competing replays either synthesize the bid/ask, as NinjaTrader and ATAS do, or use 5-minute options data like OptionNet Explorer ([NinjaTrader docs](https://docs.ninjatrader.com/ninjascript/developing_for_tick_replay); [ATAS](https://help.atas.net/en/support/solutions/articles/72000661942-replay-trading-simulator-); [Aeromir ONE](https://aeromir.com/one), about £525 a year). The replay is historical by nature, and the engineering and storage burden is itself the moat. It has the clearest B2B buyers: options educators, prop-challenge firms and brokers' education arms. It also carries the most licensing risk among the historical products, because it shows raw quotes rather than aggregates. ThetaData's written consent to display historical NBBO is the gating item. Thinkorswim OnDemand's options replay was not checked and is the most likely incumbent.

### The verifier and the observatory sell credibility where enforcement already bites

The verifier takes a claimed trade (contract, side, prices, approximate time, size). It asks whether any print occurred at that price, whether the price was inside the NBBO, whether the size exceeds what traded, and where the claim ranks among the outcomes achievable that day. For alert services it replays each alert with a realistic subscriber delay, buys at the ask and sells at the bid. This produces an "achievable" record to set beside the advertised one. Its value follows directly from the FTC's RagingBull and Warrior Trading cases, and from the gap between vendors' disclaimers and their marketing. Natural buyers are alert marketplaces and community operators who want audit badges, and subscribers vetting a service before paying. Defamation is the main risk, so output should read "consistent / inconsistent with the public tape", never "fraud". The tool also proves only that a trade was feasible, not that the poster made it.

The base-rate observatory turns the same machinery on "unusual activity". It measures how often flagged flow was followed by a move that paid after buying at the ask, compared with matched random contracts and reported with confidence intervals. It is the honest companion to Strong's finding that the UOA edge has decayed. Unusual Whales alerts from December 2023 onward are an ideal internal validation set. But publishing statistics derived from them would collide with the no-redistribution term. The public version must use a detector built independently from ThetaData prints, with UW used only for internal comparison under a commercial tier. Defensibility here is credibility: few incumbents would publish base rates that may show their own alerts are near-random. That also makes this best used as free content that drives distribution, not as a paid product.

### The backtester and GEX scorecard win only by being more honest than rivals

Retail backtesters are plentiful and several model fills beyond the mid. Option Omega uses 1-minute data with NBBO-based stops, Option Alpha has slippage settings, and tastytrade's backtester is free ([Option Omega docs](https://docs.optionomega.com/backtesting); [Option Alpha](https://optionalpha.com/blog/backtest-slippage-when-opening-and-closing-trades); [tastytrade](https://tastytrade.com/options-backtesting/)). None found offers random-entry baselines or warnings when a user has tried many parameter sets. That "statistical honesty" layer is probably what the owner already built to reach the no-edge finding, so it is the cheapest idea to productize. It is also counter-positioned: rivals profit from strategies that look good, so they are unlikely to copy it. One caveat: conditions based on UW data (for example "only enter when GEX < 0") can be tested only from December 2023, too short for regime claims, and the interface should say so.

A GEX product would compute exposure three ways: the naive open-interest convention, a version signed from flow using ThetaData prints against the attached NBBO, and Unusual Whales' per-minute series as an internal benchmark. It would show the spread across methods as an assumption band. It would also publish a running scorecard of whether the regime label predicted next-session realized intraday volatility. The academic support is real but modest and sign-dependent. Dim, Eraker and Vilkov find positive market-maker gamma is linked to lower intraday volatility and stronger reversal ([SSRN 4692190](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/Delivery.cfm/4692190.pdf?abstractid=4692190)), and Ni, Pearson, Poteshman and White document a hedge-rebalancing channel ([RFS 2021](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:34:y:2021:i:4:p:1952-1986.)). One net number per underlying, delivered end of day or delayed, is the derived output most likely to escape OPRA display treatment. That is still unconfirmed, so get it in writing. Volland already sells a once-daily tier at $150 ([user guide](https://vol.land/VollandUserGuide_Jun24.pdf)), which shows end-of-day delivery is saleable. Without Cboe's paid Open-Close data the dealer sign cannot be validated, only bracketed ([FX News Group](https://fxnewsgroup.com/forex-news/exchanges/cboe-launches-two-complementary-datasets-for-complete-visibility-into-options-trading-activity-on-c1/)). The honest pitch is "model sensitivity", not "true dealer position".

### The remaining ideas are features or funnels, not companies

A chain overlay showing round-trip cost as a share of premium, a liquidity score and a break-even that includes the spread is easy to build and easy for any broker to copy. Built as historical time-of-day spread profiles ("this contract's spread is 3× wider in the first five minutes"), or as a browser extension that computes from the quotes the user's own broker already displays, it can be given away free to feed the paid audit. Whether the extension approach complies with each broker's terms is unverified. Earnings-vol tooling is crowded: Market Chameleon and ORATS already compare implied with realized moves ([Market Chameleon](https://marketchameleon.com/EarningsReport/UpcomingEarningsAnalysis); [ORATS blog](https://orats.com/blog/meta-earnings-expected-move)). A new entrant could differ only by pricing straddles at the ask and showing full distributions over the roughly 14 years of ThetaData history, against under three years from UW alone. Behavioural flags for revenge trades, size escalation and 0DTE habits need only the user's fills, and Edgewonk and TraderSync already sell them. They belong as a bundled module. Unusual Whales' insider-trade, SEC-filing and prediction-market feeds have no evidence base in this research for a retail options use. Prediction markets are growing fast: Robinhood traded **16.4 billion event contracts in Q3 2026**, up more than 7× ([Robinhood Q3 2026](https://menafn.com/1111875312/Robinhood-Reports-Third-Quarter-2026-Results)). A tool that compares prediction-market odds with option-implied probabilities is a plausible experiment, but it is speculative and would need a UW redistribution licence to ship.

The market is real but narrow. About **5.3 million** individuals traded US options in the SEC staff count ([SEC roundtable data](https://www.sec.gov/files/roundtable-options-market-structure.pdf)), and 2025 volume hit a sixth straight record of about 15.2 billion contracts ([OCC](https://www.theocc.com/newsroom/views/2026/01-05-occ-annual-2025-and-december-2025-volume)). Robinhood Gold's 5.2 million subscribers show that mass retail pays small amounts ([Robinhood Q3 2026](https://menafn.com/1111875312/Robinhood-Reports-Third-Quarter-2026-Results)). By my arithmetic, a historical-only product at about $2,000 a month in data costs breaks even on roughly 100 subscribers at $20. A real-time product needs several hundred. Subscription revenue in this category follows market activity. Retail's share of 0DTE dropped from about 57% to 47% in the April 2025 sell-off ([Cboe](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/spx-0-dte-options-jump-to-61-share-on-retail-resurgence)). Products tied to the user's own trades, plus a B2B data leg, should hold up better than alert feeds.

## Conclusion

The licensing structure and the loss evidence point to the same product. The record of what retail actually paid sits in yesterday's tape, and yesterday's tape is the one OPRA lets a vendor redistribute without a redistribution fee. Real-time data is where the cost multiplies, where the pull toward selling signals is strongest, and where competitors already crowd. That gives a small entrant with tick-level NBBO an unusual opening: become the independent options execution auditor that Rule 605 never created, then turn the crowdsourced fill dataset into a B2B asset that brokers, advisers and researchers cannot get elsewhere. Honesty here is a strategic position, not just an ethical one. Incumbents whose marketing depends on signals looking good cannot easily copy random baselines, achievable track records or GEX error bars.

The open questions are commercial. The first is what ThetaData will charge, in writing, to display historical NBBO and derived values to subscribers. The second is whether the engaged traders who pay for tools lose enough to the spread to notice the savings. The immediate steps follow. Stop building customer-facing features on the personal keys: ThetaData's derived-works clause survives termination. Ask ThetaData sales for a historical-only display licence before writing more product code. Then measure the fill audit's effect on a pilot cohort's own spread costs before claiming any.
